What we work on

Every rep is charted.
Nothing is guesswork.

Sessions are built around your operation — steps, plant, contact, and follow-through — and every ball is logged. You leave knowing your make rate by distance and hash, not just how it felt.

01 · FIELD GOAL

The operation

Approach and steps, plant foot placement, hip rotation, strike point, and follow-through. We build a repeatable swing first, then extend range — in that order. Distance without consistency gets you cut.

02 · KICKOFF

Hang time and placement

Hang time is the number coverage units care about. We train for a live-leg approach that produces touchbacks, plus directional and sky kicks for the situations coordinators actually ask for.

03 · PUNTING

Drop, contact, control

The drop is the whole punt. Two-step operation timing, drop consistency, spiral contact, and directional work — plus pooch and rugby-style situations that keep you on the field in high school.

04 · ROUTINE

The pre-snap ritual

The best specialists do exactly the same thing before every kick, in a stadium or an empty field. We build your routine deliberately so pressure has nothing new to attack.

05 · FILM

Frame-by-frame review

Slow-motion review of your own swing against the checkpoints we're working on. Most kickers have never actually seen their plant foot land. It changes things.

06 · STRENGTH

Legs and the off-season

Guidance on the mobility, hip flexibility, and single-leg strength work that supports range — and on how many reps is too many. Overkicking injures more high school specialists than anything else.

The recruiting runway

What a specialist should be
doing, year by year

Kickers get recruited on a different calendar than everyone else on the roster, and most high school coaches have never had to learn it. Here is the map. We work this plan with every athlete.

9th grade

Build the swing

  • Establish clean mechanics before chasing distance
  • Learn the rules and the operation timing
  • Begin a mobility and single-leg strength base
  • Attend one ranking camp to get a baseline — not to compete
10th grade

Win the job

  • Become the varsity starter and get live game reps
  • First charted results at a national ranking camp
  • Start collecting film — every kick, every game
  • Build the academic profile college coaches will ask for
11th grade

The recruiting year

  • This is the year that matters most for specialists
  • Camp circuit, junior days, and campus visits
  • Register with the NCAA Eligibility Center
  • Direct outreach to special teams coordinators, with film
  • Consistent charted range at the distances your level requires
12th grade

Decide honestly

  • Evaluate offers, preferred walk-on spots, and roster promises
  • Understand what a "camp invite" actually means
  • Signing periods and the paperwork that follows
  • Real talk about prep year, JUCO, and the portal era

Questions parents ask

Straight answers

When should a kicker start training?

Middle school is fine for building mechanics, but the work that changes a recruiting outcome starts in ninth and tenth grade. Waiting until junior year is not fatal — it just removes your margin for error, because eleventh grade is when college coaches are actually looking.

Do college coaches really recruit specialists?

Yes, but the market is small and it rewards preparation over raw leg. A typical Division I program signs a scholarship kicker once every four or five years, and fills the rest with preferred walk-ons. That is not a reason to quit — it is a reason to be organized earlier than everyone else.

Can you guarantee a scholarship?

No, and you should be careful with anyone who does. What we can do is make sure your mechanics hold up under pressure, your film is built the way a special teams coordinator wants to watch it, and your target list is honest about your level. The rest is your leg and your grades.

How often should we train?

For most high school specialists, once a week in season and once or twice a week in the off-season, with structured work on your own between sessions. More is usually worse. Overkicking is the most common cause of hip and groin injuries in young specialists.

Do you work with punters and long snappers?

Punters, yes — punting is trained the same way, starting with the drop. Long snapping is a genuinely separate craft, and you are better served by a dedicated snapping coach. Ask and we will point you toward one.

My son plays soccer. Is that an advantage?

Often, yes. Soccer players arrive with the hip mobility and the striking instinct already built, and the transition is mostly about learning a new operation and a new plant. Laith came the same way.
