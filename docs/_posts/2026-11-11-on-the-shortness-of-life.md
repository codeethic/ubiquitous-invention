**We're not short on time. We're just terrible at spending it.**

*Reflections on Seneca, mortality, and the business of actually living.*

Nearly 2,000 years ago, the Roman philosopher Seneca wrote an essay called *On the Shortness of Life*. His central argument was deceptively simple: life isn't necessarily too short. We just waste an extraordinary amount of it.

It's a provocative idea, particularly when you're old enough to look backward and recognize just how much of your life has been spent doing things that, in retrospect, probably didn't matter all that much.

Seneca wasn't particularly interested in how many years we have. He was interested in what we do with them.

And that's where things get uncomfortable.

## The illusion of having plenty of time

We behave as though time is an unlimited resource.

We postpone vacations until work settles down. We delay difficult conversations until the timing feels right. We tell ourselves we'll spend more time with family when things aren't so busy. We'll pursue the things we're passionate about when we retire.

Tomorrow becomes next week. Next week becomes next year.

Eventually, we look up and realize that the years have been passing while we've been making other plans.

Seneca observed that we are remarkably protective of our money, possessions, and property, yet casually surrender our most valuable resource: time.

We lend it to employers, give it to trivial obligations, spend it worrying about things outside our control, and sacrifice it trying to impress people whose opinions won't matter in a decade.

And then we wonder where it went.

## Being busy isn't the same as being alive

One of Seneca's most enduring criticisms was directed at people who are perpetually occupied.

He called them busy lives, but not necessarily meaningful ones.

There's a distinction between being productive and being fulfilled. Between accomplishing things and experiencing life.

Modern society has become particularly good at confusing the two.

We measure ourselves by professional accomplishments, compensation, promotions, possessions, investment balances, and the number of things we manage to squeeze into a day.

We optimize everything.

Our careers. Our finances. Our bodies. Our calendars.

But to what end?

At some point, the pursuit of a better life can become the very thing preventing us from enjoying the one we already have.

There's something absurd about spending 40 years accumulating enough resources to finally enjoy the freedom to do what we could have been doing all along.

## The problem with waiting for retirement

This passage of Seneca's thinking hits particularly hard when considering retirement.

We spend decades exchanging time for money, often convincing ourselves that the sacrifice is temporary.

We'll travel later.

We'll spend more time with our children later.

We'll pursue hobbies later.

We'll slow down later.

Retirement becomes this imaginary finish line where life finally begins.

But retirement isn't guaranteed. Neither is good health. Neither is another 20 or 30 years.

And even if we get there, we've already surrendered an enormous portion of our lives.

Financial independence is valuable precisely because it gives us back control over our time. But there's a danger in turning financial independence into another endless pursuit.

There will always be another dollar to accumulate, another percentage point to optimize, another investment decision to debate.

At some point, enough has to mean enough.

Otherwise, we've simply traded one occupation for another.

## The past, present, and future

Seneca makes an interesting observation about how people experience time.

Some are consumed by regret over the past. Others are consumed by anxiety about the future. Very few seem capable of appreciating the present.

The past cannot be changed. The future cannot be guaranteed.

Yet we routinely sacrifice today worrying about tomorrow.

We revisit mistakes, replay conversations, wonder what might have been, and spend enormous energy anticipating problems that may never materialize.

Meanwhile, the actual experience of being alive continues happening around us.

Our children grow up. Friendships evolve. People we love disappear. Opportunities pass.

And we miss some of it because we're somewhere else mentally.

I suspect this is one of the hardest lessons to internalize. It's relatively easy to understand intellectually. It's considerably harder to practice.

## Spend your time like it matters

Seneca wasn't arguing that everyone should abandon their responsibilities, quit working, or spend their days pursuing pleasure.

He was arguing for intentionality.

Know what matters. Be deliberate about where your attention goes. Stop surrendering your life to obligations that don't deserve it.

Spend time with people who make you better.

Read. Learn. Explore. Create.

Have the difficult conversations.

Take the trip.

Tell people you love them while they're still around to hear it.

And perhaps most importantly, stop waiting for some future version of yourself to begin living.

## The uncomfortable conclusion

There's a line of thinking in Seneca's essay that has stayed with me: it's not that we have too little time, but that we lose so much of it.

I think about that differently as I get older.

I think about my boys and how quickly childhood became adulthood. I think about friends we've lost, conversations we won't have again, and experiences that seemed ordinary at the time but have become some of the most meaningful memories of my life.

I think about the years spent working, saving, planning, and preparing for a future that was never guaranteed.

And I wonder how much of it was necessary.

Not because work is meaningless. Not because ambition is inherently bad. Not because financial security doesn't matter.

But because there is a point where the pursuit of a better tomorrow begins to cannibalize today.

Seneca's essay isn't really about death. It's about the life we're wasting while pretending we have an unlimited supply of it.

We don't.

None of us knows how much time remains. We only know that whatever remains is less than what we've already spent.

So perhaps the objective isn't to live longer.

It's to be more present for the life we actually have.

Less time worrying about what comes next.

Less time chasing things that don't matter.

More time with the people who do.

**Life isn't short. Our willingness to waste it is what makes it feel that way.**

*And that's probably worth thinking about before we spend another day putting off the things that make being alive worthwhile.*
