01
The order of operations

Starting at 23? Do these money moves in this order

The internet is full of money advice, and most of it argues about step six while beginners are stuck on step one. There's a settled order of operations that answers "what should I do with my next dollar?" — and following the sequence matters more than optimizing any single piece. Each step earns the right to the next.

Check off what you've already handled and the tool points to your next move. Notice where investing actually sits — not first. A safety net and killing high-interest debt come before pouring money into the market: a 22% credit-card balance is a guaranteed 22% loss no investment can reliably beat.

Why order beats optimizationEach early step is a guaranteed win: an emergency fund stops a surprise from becoming credit-card debt, the employer match is free money, and paying off a 20%+ balance is a risk-free 20% return. Only after those does the market — with its uncertainty — become the best home for a dollar. Get the order right and the rest is tuning.
Does income change the order?The sequence barely moves with income — a Roth IRA and HSA come before finishing the 401(k) because they usually offer cheaper, broader investments (and the HSA is triple-tax-free), which is true at any salary. What income changes is how you run the Roth-IRA step. Direct Roth IRA contributions phase out in 2026 at a MAGI of $153k–$168k (single) and $242k–$252k (married filing jointly); above that you use a "backdoor Roth" (contribute to a nondeductible traditional IRA, then convert). The HSA has no income limit. Income also flips the flavor: a low bracket — like a 23-year-old's — favors Roth (pay tax cheaply now), while peak-earning high brackets often favor pre-tax contributions (deduct now, pay later at a lower rate).
QYou're 23 with a small cash cushion, a 22% credit-card balance, and a 401(k) that offers a match. After grabbing the full match, where does the next dollar do the most good?
Kill the high-interest debt first. Paying off a 22% balance is a guaranteed, tax-free 22% return — better than the stock market's long-run average and with none of the risk. Grab the match (free money), then clear high-interest debt before investing further.
02
Habits & the cost of waiting

At 23 your biggest advantage isn't money — it's time

A 23-year-old can't out-save a 40-year-old, but they don't have to: a dollar invested now has four extra decades to compound, and compounding is exponential, so those early dollars do wildly more work than later ones. The catch is that the benefit is invisible for years — which is exactly why so many people wait, and why waiting is so expensive. Slide the start age below and watch what each delayed year costs.

The other half of the game is behavioral, and it's simple to say and hard to do: automate your contributions so saving isn't a monthly decision, keep buying through downturns instead of panic-selling, ignore hot-stock and crypto hype, and don't let lifestyle creep eat every raise. Boring and consistent beats clever and sporadic.

The habits that actually decide itSet contributions to happen automatically on payday, raise them whenever your income rises, and then do nothing during scary markets — the investors who bail at the bottom lock in the loss. You will never time the market; you don't need to. Consistency over 40 years is the whole trick.
QAna invests $300/month from age 25, Ben invests the same from age 35, both to 65 at the same return. Roughly how do they compare at 65?
Ana wins by a landslide. Her early dollars compound the longest, so a 10-year head start often doubles the ending balance even though she only contributed for 10 more years. Time in the market, not timing the market, is the young saver's superpower.
03
The number — are you on track?

Two questions decide everything: how big a pile, and are you on pace?

How much is enough? The quickest honest answer comes from flipping the 4% rule on its head: if a portfolio can safely provide about 4% a year, then you need roughly 25 times the annual spending you want it to cover. Hoping for $60,000 a year from your investments? Your target is somewhere near $1.5 million.

The encouraging part is that you don't get there on contributions alone — compounding does most of the lifting, and the earlier you start, the bigger its share of the final pile. Set your numbers below and watch how much of the ending balance is growth versus money you actually set aside.

04
The funding waterfall

Where each saved dollar should go — and why the order isn't equal

Once you're saving, the next question is where each dollar lands — and the tiers are not created equal. The very top of the list isn't an investment at all: it's your employer's match, an instant 50–100% return no market can promise. Skipping it is the one money mistake with no upside.

A widely used priority: capture the full match first, then fund an HSA if you're eligible (the only triple-tax-free account), then max your Roth/IRA and the rest of your 401(k), and finally a taxable brokerage for anything left over. (Before any of it: a starter emergency fund and any high-interest debt.) Slide your yearly savings up and watch the buckets fill in order.

Don't leave the raise on the tableThe match is a guaranteed return you can't get anywhere else, so it comes before paying off a low-rate mortgage or investing elsewhere. After the match, the HSA is the most underused tier — contribute, invest it, and pay medical bills from cash so it compounds tax-free for decades, becoming a stealth retirement account.
QYour employer matches 50% of contributions up to 6% of pay, but you can only save a little this year. Where should the first dollars go?
Always grab the match first. A 50% match is an instant 50% return on those dollars — nothing else in the waterfall, and no investment, reliably beats that. Capture the full match, then work your way down to the HSA, Roth/401(k), and finally taxable.
05
What to buy & fees

Own the whole market — and guard every basis point

Once you're contributing, what do you actually buy? For most people the answer is delightfully boring: a low-cost index fund that owns essentially the entire market at once. Trying to pick individual winners is a game most professionals lose — after fees, the majority of active funds trail a simple index over long periods. Owning everything cheaply beats guessing.

And watch the one cost you fully control: the expense ratio. It sounds trivial — 1% versus 0.05% — but fees compound in reverse, quietly, every single year. Over a 40-year horizon that gap can eat a startling share of your final balance. Slide the fee up and watch the drain.

The cheapest reliable edgeYou can't control the market, but you can control your costs — and low fees are the closest thing to a free lunch in investing. QOver 40 years, how much can a 1% annual expense ratio (versus about 0.05%) cost a portfolio?
Fees compound like returns, in reverse. Roughly 1% skimmed every year for decades can quietly erase a fifth or more of the ending balance. It's the one cost you fully control — which is why low-cost index funds are the default answer.
06
Asset allocation & the glide path

Your stock-and-bond mix sets both your growth and your nerves

The split between stocks and bonds is the biggest investing decision you make — it sets your expected growth and how rough the ride is. Stocks have paid more over long stretches but swing hard; bonds are steadier but grow slowly. There's no free lunch: reaching for more return means accepting a wider range of outcomes.

That's why the mix should change with time. Early on, decades of compounding can ride out the swings, so a stock-heavy portfolio makes sense. As retirement nears, a single bad year matters far more — you met this as sequence risk — so the standard move is to glide toward more bonds. Target-date funds do exactly this on autopilot. Set a mix and a time horizon and watch the cone of outcomes widen and shift.

A starting rule, then adjustA common heuristic is to hold roughly (110 − your age)% in stocks — about 80% at 30, 50% at 60 — then tune it to your own stomach for risk. QWhy do target-date funds shift from mostly stocks toward more bonds as you near retirement?
It's about protecting the landing. Early on, time smooths the swings; near retirement, a crash you're forced to sell into can do permanent damage. Gliding toward bonds trades a little growth for stability exactly when a bad sequence would hurt most.
07
Inflation — the silent tax

Why "safe" cash is the risky choice over decades

There's one force working against your money the entire time: inflation. Prices drift up a few percent a year, so the same dollar buys a little less each year — and over a long horizon "a little less" compounds into "a lot less." At about 3% a year, a dollar loses half its purchasing power in roughly 24 years. That's why the real target isn't a big number on a statement; it's buying power.

This flips the usual intuition about risk. Cash feels safe because the number never drops — but left alone for decades it quietly bleeds value, guaranteed. Investments swing around, yet historically they've outpaced inflation over long stretches. Keep cash for near-term needs and emergencies; for money you won't touch for years, standing still is the real danger. Watch both paths below.

QAt about 3% inflation, roughly how long until a dollar's buying power is cut in half?
Roughly 24 years. The Rule of 72 says 72 ÷ 3 ≈ 24 years to halve buying power. It's why cash sitting idle for decades quietly loses, and why long-term money has to outpace inflation to truly grow.

Tax buckets — the foundation

Not every dollar you save is taxed the same way

Picture yourself right now — still working, a paycheck coming in, and some money left over to save. Where that money goes decides when the government taxes it. Every retirement dollar lands in one of three tax buckets, and each bucket taxes you at a different moment: on the way in, along the way, or on the way out.

Getting this straight is the whole foundation. Once you see that a traditional 401(k) and a Roth are the same investment with opposite tax timing, everything else — conversions, RMDs, withdrawal order — becomes a question of which bucket to fill and which to tap.

The strategy in one lineNobody knows their exact future tax rates, so the durable move is tax diversification — build up all three buckets while you're working. QYou contribute to both a traditional 401(k) and a Roth 401(k) this year. What's the core difference in how they're taxed?
That's the whole idea. Traditional = pay later (deduct now, ordinary income at withdrawal, and RMDs eventually). Roth = pay now (after-tax going in, tax-free coming out, no RMDs). Same investment, opposite tax timing — which is exactly why holding some of each gives you options in retirement.

Roth conversions & brackets

Income fills brackets like water fills a glass

The U.S. uses a progressive income tax. That word gets misread constantly, so here is the whole idea in one sentence: your income is poured into a stack of buckets, each bucket has its own tax rate, and a dollar is taxed at the rate of whichever bucket it lands in — not the rate of the top bucket you reach.

Move the slider and watch the buckets fill. The marginal rate is the rate on your next dollar — the bucket the water is touching right now. The effective rate is the tax you actually pay divided by your whole income — always lower, because your first dollars were taxed cheaply.

QYou're in the 22% bracket with $18,000 of room left before the 24% bracket begins. You convert $30,000 to Roth. How is that conversion taxed?
Right — only the spillover is taxed higher. Filling the remaining $18,000 of the 22% bucket costs 22%. Only the $12,000 that spills over is taxed at 24%. This is exactly why "convert up to the top of a bracket" is such a common strategy.
10
RMDs & the tax time-bomb

The tax you deferred doesn't disappear — it compounds

Traditional 401(k)s and IRAs are a deal with the government: skip the tax today, pay it whenever you withdraw. For decades that feels like a gift. But the balance keeps growing, and eventually the IRS wants its share — so at age 75 it starts requiring withdrawals whether you need the money or not. These are Required Minimum Distributions, and each one is ordinary taxable income.

The trap is compounding. A bigger balance forces a bigger RMD, which can push you into higher brackets, inflate the taxable portion of Social Security, and even raise your Medicare premiums. Grow the balance below and watch the required withdrawal — and the tax under it — swell year after year.

Why 75 mattersThe first RMD divides your balance by about 24.6 — roughly 4%. Converting to Roth in your 60s and early 70s — the low-income "gap years" before RMDs and often before Social Security — shrinks the balance that gets hit.
QWhy are the years between retiring and age 75 often the best time for Roth conversions?
Exactly. Those "gap years" frequently have little earned income and no RMDs yet, so the low brackets sit empty. Filling them with conversions is cheap — and it permanently shrinks the pre-tax balance that would otherwise drive ever-larger RMDs later.
11
Social Security & taxes

How much of your Social Security is taxed is up to your other income

Most people are surprised that Social Security benefits can be taxed at all. Whether they are — and how much — depends on a special number called provisional income: your other income plus any tax-exempt interest, plus half of your Social Security benefit. Cross the first threshold and up to 50% of your benefit becomes taxable; cross the second and up to 85% does.

Here's the sting worth knowing about. In the ramp zones, every extra $1 you pull from a traditional IRA doesn't just get taxed itself — it can also drag $0.50 to $0.85 of your Social Security into taxable territory. So your real marginal rate on that dollar can be 1.5× to 1.85× your bracket. Advisors call it the tax torpedo.

Why this rewards planningBecause ordinary income both gets taxed and pulls your benefit into the tax base, the fixes are the same ones you've met: build a Roth bucket so some retirement income doesn't count toward provisional income, and do conversions in low-income years before you claim Social Security, while there's no benefit to torpedo.
QYou're in the Social Security "ramp" zone. You take an extra $1,000 from your traditional IRA. Why can that feel like it's taxed at more than your bracket?
That's the torpedo. In the ramp zone each extra dollar of ordinary income can make $0.50–$0.85 of benefit taxable on top of itself. A retiree in the 12% bracket can face a true marginal rate of 18–22% on those dollars — a strong reason to fill low brackets with Roth conversions before claiming.
12
When to claim Social Security

The same benefit, worth very different amounts depending on when you start

You can start Social Security any time from 62 to 70, and the age you pick permanently sets the size of every check. Claim early and each payment is smaller — as little as 70% of your full benefit at 62. Wait past your full retirement age (67 for anyone born in 1960 or later) and it grows about 8% for every year you delay, up to 124% at 70. Same earnings record, wildly different monthly checks.

So which wins? It's a bet on longevity. Claiming early means more checks but smaller ones; waiting means fewer but bigger. They cross at a break-even age — usually the early-to-mid 80s. Live past it and waiting pays more in total; die before it and claiming early came out ahead. Slide the claim age and your life expectancy and watch the lines cross.

Delaying is cheap longevity insuranceWaiting to 70 buys you the largest possible inflation-adjusted check for the rest of your life — the best protection against the real risk of outliving your money. The ACA subsidy cliff

Retire before 65? Your income sets your health-insurance subsidy

Retire before Medicare kicks in at 65 and you'll likely buy coverage on the ACA marketplace — where the premium subsidy you get is scored entirely on your MAGI (modified adjusted gross income) measured against the Federal Poverty Level for your household. The higher your income, the smaller the subsidy.

And there's an edge. The enhanced subsidies that softened this from 2021 through 2025 have expired, so in 2026 the old 400%-of-poverty cliff is back: one dollar of income over the line and the entire subsidy vanishes at once. For an early retiree that single dollar can cost thousands. Drag the income slider through the edge and watch.

The lever you controlMAGI is manageable. QAn early retiree's MAGI is $1 below 400% of poverty and they're getting a $6,000 subsidy. They do a Roth conversion that pushes MAGI $500 over the line (in a year with the cliff in effect). What happens to the subsidy?
That's the cliff. With the enhanced subsidies expired, crossing 400% of poverty forfeits the whole premium tax credit at once — it's reconciled on your tax return, so a small overage can trigger a large repayment. This is why early retirees watch every source of MAGI in marketplace years.

Sequence risk & safe withdrawal

The order of returns can matter more than the average

While you're saving, the order of good and bad years barely matters — you're not touching the money, so it all averages out. The moment you start withdrawing, order becomes everything. A crash in your first few retirement years forces you to sell more shares at low prices to fund the same spending, and those shares aren't around to recover. This is sequence-of-returns risk.
